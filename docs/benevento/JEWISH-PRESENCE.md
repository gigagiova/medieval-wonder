# Benevento: Jewish community, quarter and proposed synagogue site

Research checked 26 October 2026. This note distinguishes the date of an event from the date of the surviving source, and the later Jewish quarter from an AD 700 reconstruction. No map, history table or building geometry is changed by this note.

## What can presently be established

**Benevento had a Jewish community and a later identifiable Jewish quarter.** The municipality places the quarter between **Piano di Corte and Porta Somma** in its account of the sixteenth-century expulsion. A local historical association places the *giudecca* between **Piazza Piano di Corte and Santa Sofia**, and associates the former **Santo Stefano de Neophitis**, in the alley opposite the Basilica of San Bartolomeo, with a **presumed** synagogue. These are usable leads for later historical topography, not evidence that this synagogue or these quarter boundaries already existed in 700.

The earliest explicit community narrative actually read in this pass concerns **mid-ninth-century events in an eleventh-century chronicle**. Earlier origins are suggested in modern summaries, but this pass did not recover a securely dated fifth-, sixth- or seventh-century Beneventan Jewish inscription, charter or synagogue excavation. This is a statement about the evidence retrieved, not a claim that such evidence cannot exist.

## Sources read and what each establishes

### 1. Chronicle of Ahimaaz: early community evidence, not a street address

Ahimaaz ben Paltiel, *The Chronicle of Ahimaaz*, translated with introduction and notes by **Marcus Salzman**, Columbia University Press, 1924. Public-domain scan and OCR retrieved from [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/chronicleofahima00ahim), [full OCR](https://archive.org/download/chronicleofahima00ahim/chronicleofahima00ahim_djvu.txt).

- Introduction dates composition to **1054** (printed p. 9; OCR context checked).
- Printed pp. **63–65** describe Abu Aaron's visit: “When he reached Beneventum the entire community came out as one man to welcome him.” The following episode describes Sabbath communal prayer and a prayer leader.
- Salzman's note on p. **63** places Aaron's teaching in southern Italy around the **middle of the ninth century**. This date is the editor's historical interpretation; it is not an AD 850 dated inscription.
- The episode is explicitly miraculous: Aaron recognizes a prayer leader as a dead man. It is evidence for the chronicle's remembered community and religious life, not literal verification of the supernatural story.
- Printed p. **92** also describes Hananeel's welcome by the community, settlement in Beneventum and marriage to Esther, daughter of R. Shabbethai.
- Neither retrieved passage locates a synagogue, defines quarter boundaries, or supplies architecture that can be reconstructed for 700.

David Kaufmann's earlier *Die Chronik des Achimaaz von Oria (850–1054)* (1896) was also retrieved through [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/diechronikdesac00kaufgoog). Its discussion of Aaron at Benevento confirms that this is a longstanding reading of the chronicle, not an independent contemporary ninth-century record.

**Classification:** retrospective medieval literary evidence for a community in the ninth-century narrative; insufficient by itself for a securely attested community site or synagogue in 700. Existing `PEOPLE.md` shorthand “documented from the 9th century” should be understood with this source-date qualification.

### 2. Comune di Benevento: later quarter location and dated civic history

[Comune di Benevento, “La Storia”](https://comune.benevento.it/vivere-il-comune/la-storia/), municipal historical overview, full page retrieved and read.

The account of Pius V's expulsion explicitly says that Jewish citizens had to abandon the quarter:

> “il ghetto, che si trovava fra Piano di Corte e Porta Somma.”

It also reports:

- **1374:** Bishop Ugo Guilardi ordered priests not to use coercion or blackmail to induce Beneventan Jews to accept baptism.
- **22 May 1617:** the city consuls proposed asking Pope Paul V for Jewish residents to return; council approved with **27 votes to 5**. The overview says archival documents do not record the return.

This is an official modern synthesis, not a transcription or facsimile of those records. It does not date the quarter's foundation and does not identify a seventh-century synagogue. Its word *ghetto* in a later-period narrative must not be projected automatically onto early medieval settlement or assumed to prove a walled compulsory enclosure in 700.

**Classification:** credible municipal attribution of the later quarter's broad location; dated later history reported by a secondary source. Not an exact footprint or AD 700 boundary.

### 3. Isidea statement: precise proposed synagogue location, explicitly uncertain

[NTR24, 7 August 2016, “La proposta di Isidea: ‘L'ex chiesa di Santo Stefano diventi un museo ebraico’”](https://www.ntr24.tv/2016/08/07/la-proposta-di-isidea-lex-chiesa-di-santo-stefano-diventi-un-museo-ebraico/). This article publishes a statement by **Rito Martignetti**, president of the local association **Isidea**. It is a heritage proposal quoting historical work, not an excavation report.

Relevant statements, separated from their evidential limits:

- The later *giudecca* is described as a “quartiere preferenziale” between **Piazza Piano di Corte and the Santa Sofia complex**. This is not necessarily inconsistent with the municipality's broader Piano di Corte–Porta Somma description, but the two wordings are not a surveyed boundary polygon.
- The statement says the **Obituarium Sancti Spiritus, compiled in 1198**, documents **S. Nazarius a Judeca** (area of today's Piano di Corte), **S. Januarius de Judeca** (near Porta Somma), and **S. Stephanus de Judeca / S. Stephanus in plano Curie**. It then discusses **Santo Stefano de Neophitis**, “cioè dei convertiti,” in the **alley opposite the Basilica of San Bartolomeo**. The obituary itself was not retrieved, and the article's sequence of aliases must not be treated as independently proven identity of every named church.
- For the former Santo Stefano building, the article's actual language is **“la presunta sinagoga”** — **the presumed synagogue**. Therefore it does **not** establish a confirmed synagogue conversion, foundation date, or surviving Jewish fabric.
- Citing De Nicastro, it describes a two-nave church, damaged in the **1688 earthquake**, then rebuilt with one nave. A bell was blessed in **1695**; the parish was transferred to San Bartolomeo by municipal decision on **6 July 1866**. These much later church forms cannot be used as an AD 700 synagogue design.
- It reports a Hebrew funerary inscription for **Samuel son of Isaac**, dated **4913 / 1153**, found in **1898** and kept in the **Museo del Sannio**; the associated cemetery in **Cretarossa** is explicitly described as hypothesized. This is a concrete lead for dated epigraphic confirmation, but the stone/catalogue was not independently inspected in this pass.
- It cites **Cesare Colafemmina, “Gli ebrei in Benevento,”** presented at the **1995 Italia Judaica conference in Tel Aviv**, and says he discussed evidence in the Lombard period. The article does not give a seventh-century charter or exact synagogue plan. The conference paper was not obtained; do not claim it was read.
- An earlier origin in the time of the **Via Appia Traiana** is attributed to an **Alfredo Zazo hypothesis**. The article itself calls it a hypothesis; it cannot certify Roman or AD 700 continuity.

**Classification:** verified modern source for the location of a *proposed* synagogue identification; later church and quarter evidence. Synagogue identity remains an attributed hypothesis.

### 4. Other cross-checks and conflicting summaries

- [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Benevento,” article 2939](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/2939-benevento), retrieved. This early twentieth-century entry reports a community in **1165** and includes **P. M. Lonardo, “Gli Ebrei a Benevento” (1899)** in its bibliography. It does not give the required synagogue footprint. Its discussion of 1165 does not disprove the chronicle's earlier community narrative.
- [JGuide Europe, Benevento](https://jguideeurope.org/en/region/italy/campania/benevento/), retrieved. It cautiously says presence “seems” to date to at least the **fifth century**, mentions Hananeel ben Paltiel and an eleventh-century yeshiva, and reports twelfth-century inscriptions. It provides no primary citation for the fifth-century claim and no synagogue address. Therefore do not replace the ninth-century literary evidence with a supposedly certain fifth-century foundation.
- The JGuide page says **200 families** for Benjamin of Tudela; the Isidea statement says **200 Jews**. The parent investigation directly checked Marcus Nathan Adler’s 1907 translation of the itinerary ([public-domain text](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/The-Itinerary-of-Benjamin-of-Tudela_14981/master/14981.txt)), which says “about 200 Jews.” The disagreement with summaries saying families must not be silently normalized or used to size a quarter.
- [NTR24, 10 April 2012, historical walk](https://www.ntr24.tv/2012/04/10/passeggiata-nel-centro-storico-di-benevento-sulle-tracce-degli-ebrei/) confirms a local guided interpretation of the Jewish quarter, led by Nicola Del Basso, but supplies no dating or useful map coordinates.

## Implications for the AD 700 map

- Retain a distinction between **Jewish people/community plausibility**, **a later documented quarter**, and **a particular synagogue building**. One does not prove the others.
- A historical annotation may identify the later quarter around Piano di Corte, with the sources' separate eastern reference points and period clearly stated. Do not mark the entire area as a securely attested Jewish quarter in 700.
- A synagogue at the presumed Santo Stefano site in 700 would currently be a **reconstruction hypothesis**, not “attested.” No retrieved source supplies an AD 700 footprint, dimensions, orientation, roof or interior. Do not copy the post-1688 church or fabricate archaeological certainty.
- Lack of retrieved AD 700 synagogue evidence is **not** grounds to claim Jews were absent. Conversely, regional Jewish evidence from Venosa or Oria is not direct proof of a Benevento building.
- The next decisive work is to inspect Colafemmina's paper and its primary references, the 1198 obituary edition, and the Museo del Sannio inscription catalogue. Those can refine dates and locations; they should precede a confident synagogue reconstruction.

## Implemented reconstruction: small prayer room

The owner explicitly authorized plausible reconstruction where exact AD 700
evidence is missing, with uncertainty retained here rather than exposed as
in-game labels. A small synagogue now occupies an existing explorable urban
lot near Piano di Corte/Santa Sofia. This is not a claimed excavation footprint
of Santo Stefano, nor a surveyed AD 700 Jewish-quarter boundary. Other registered
building footprints and streets are retained.

The domestic-scale room has perimeter benches, a central scroll-reading table,
a plain timber scroll cupboard on the available wall nearest the direction of
Jerusalem, and oil lamps. These functions draw on the
[Ostia archaeological park's synagogue interpretation](https://ostiaantica.cultura.gov.it/pannelli-didattici-e-mappe/i-quartieri-costieri-e-le-domus-marittime/sinagoga/),
an earlier Italian analogue, not proof of identical Beneventan furnishings.
No monumental Ostian plan, modern flags, compulsory enclosure, or church altar
is copied. The existing lot shell supplies modest masonry and timber construction.

The readable quotes Deuteronomy 6:4 in unpointed Hebrew, with an English
translation. Its textual attestation does not attest the room's reconstructed
location. A local Jewish resident makes the place part of the inhabited street.
